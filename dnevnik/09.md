## In God’s Fashion

*A conversation in the corridor*

“What do you think of the way our members of parliament work? Is it good or bad?”
“In God’s fashion.”

“Is that good or bad?”
“It’s terrible.”
“What’s the connection?”
“Think of God—or the gods—creating human beings. How many gods were there?”
“One.”
“One to conceive the design, one to carry it out, one to judge the result—all the same one. And here we are. Now imagine there were three.”

“As in Christianity?”
“Yes. One conceived the design, another carried it out, and a third assessed the result. Would that third one have approved this product for manufacture?”

“I don’t think so. Never.”

“Why not?”
“The engineering is questionable. Just consider the decision to route breathing, drinking, eating, chewing and swallowing through a single throat. And what do you make of combining peeing, defecating and sex in the same area?”

“Yes, if there had been three—one to design, another to build, and a third to assess the result objectively…”

“Then the third would never have approved us for mass production.”

“And that is how our members of parliament work: they devise it themselves, implement it themselves and judge it themselves, purely by eye—in God’s fashion.”

“If we did things in a properly human way, such a shambles would never be accepted.”
“So how should it work?”

“One designs, another implements, and a third measures and checks. Implementation must be entrusted to a professional; the politician must remain responsible for the underlying purpose; and a neutral expert must measure the results, together with the politician who initiated the cycle. The politician’s idea can then be developed further, and the cycle repeated.”

## Barriers that change the very nature of the process

One can criticise the Knesset for its lack of separation of powers and of checks and balances. But the problem is much worse. A member of parliament’s work is not merely a sack race. It is a hurdles race in which every hurdle pushes the runner either to trade noble public goals for petty, immediate personal interests—or to drop out altogether. This is the root of every failure, from October 7 to Flydubai: formally, the government is in charge. In reality, there is no one at the controls.

**Here are four barriers that change everything.**

**First.** Members of parliament are immediately divided into opposition and coalition. We begin with war instead of cooperation. If the split is 62 to 58, only four members can work; the other 116 are fighting.

**Second.** What is chosen is neither a strategy nor a plan, but a person to be prime minister. Clear thinking and persuasive speech are not what counts. What matters is being on friendly terms with everyone—one hand washes the other.

**Third.** A prime minister who loses a majority can be out in a day. The planning horizon is therefore a single day. You can steal something in a day. You cannot build anything in a day.

**Fourth.** To stay in office, the prime minister trades ministerial portfolios for loyalty. Government ceases to be government and becomes a bargaining chip in a market for petty vanity, political influence and social status.

Congratulations to those elected! The transformation of people with a mission into petty traders in conscience is complete.

Have a pleasant flight!

## The problem and the solution

Under the existing rules, rational government is impossible. Every catastrophe that has befallen us—or nearly befallen us—is rooted in these rules.

Here is a proposal for a different set of rules that could guarantee rational government.

- There is no opposition and no governing coalition. There are members of parliament, whose missions (“I will fight for…”) are published. These missions inevitably conflict with one another.
- An individual member of parliament need not see the whole picture, so an overarching policy framework is needed to balance these missions. Experienced members propose their own frameworks. Parliament selects the best one. The proposed frameworks are published and open for comment.
- The author of the winning framework becomes prime minister and may appoint several members of parliament as deputies. Together, they form the government’s political front end. The prime minister recruits the ministers. The government’s executive back end—the ministers and their staff—may not be members of parliament.
- The members of parliament who do not join the prime minister’s team act collectively as the commissioning body. They elect a counterpart to the prime minister to coordinate their work. They also recruit independent experts.
- Before the government begins work, the policy framework must be translated into a specification of objectives and deliverables. To do this, the experts and ministers jointly draw up a list of measurable system indicators, recording their current values and the values to be achieved in six months. This specification is published and open for comment.
- The government works without interference for six months, with an interim report after three months.
- After six months, the experts and ministers check the new values of the system indicators.
- The experts and members of parliament evaluate the government’s performance.
- Parliament decides whether to continue working with this prime minister or elect a new one.
- Rules for force majeure situations must be set out separately.

## On resistance and overcoming it

Today, particular ministries are effectively controlled by particular parties. This is incompatible with the proposed rules, under which the prime minister is responsible for the performance of the entire government and therefore selects the ministers personally. Resistance is inevitable, however. Ministerial portfolios mean influence, appointments and budgets. They are hard to give up.

Yet the principle itself is archaic. In the Middle Ages, lords rewarded their vassals’ support with lands and offices from which they could draw an income. Today, areas of public administration are allocated in return for political support. We want to move to an industrial principle: **objective → resources → professional implementer → measurable result.** The aim of the reform is not to take influence away from some and give it to others, but to improve the effectiveness of government and thereby enlarge the overall “pie”.
